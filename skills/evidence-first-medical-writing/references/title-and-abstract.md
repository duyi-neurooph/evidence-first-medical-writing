# Titles and abstracts that stand alone

The title identifies the question. The abstract answers it, or, for a protocol, explains how it will be answered. Neither should need the other to prevent a material misreading. Use the target journal's current article-type rules, not the typography, catchphrases or omissions of a published example.

## Title: what would a reader expect was actually studied?

Read the title **alone**, excluding keywords, abstract and notes. Paraphrase the question in one sentence. Separate what is stated from what a reader might assume about the population, comparison, measured outcome and design. Which alternative interpretation would matter? Could a closely related but scientifically different paper use the same title unchanged? A missing detail is not itself a defect.

Then compare that reading with the confirmed design and source facts. Fix the fewest words that distinguish the actual study: an invitation rather than a procedure, maintenance rather than initial treatment, a measured score rather than clinical events, or a secondary analysis rather than independent randomized evidence. Use PICO-T to **choose**, not to force every element into a title. For a primary randomized trial report, identify randomization in the title (e.g., “a randomized trial”; [CONSORT item 1a](https://www.consort-spirit.org/item-1a-identification)). Do not confer that label on an analysis just because its data came from a trial. Use a neutral question/comparison rather than promising benefit. Where a verified journal requirement conflicts with reporting guidance, flag the conflict to the author; don't silently drop a design distinction.

When drafting, use confirmed facts to build one concise title first and compare alternatives only when they convey meaningfully different emphases or the user requests them. An accurate, clear title can be kept. Don't add numbers, setting, timing or qualifiers that do not resolve a material ambiguity or satisfy a requirement.

## Abstract: what answer can the reader recover without the paper?

Read the abstract as a separate unit. Without borrowing a definition from the title, tables or body, state the clinical question, actual comparison or reference standard, and the answer. For empirical results identify the relevant population, endpoint and time, group or denominator, magnitude, direction and uncertainty as applicable. Can the reader tell **higher than what**, whether a higher score is better or worse, and whether a measure represents an event, a surrogate or a prediction?

When drafting, make the objective name the unresolved choice, Methods explain how it was tested, Results report the prespecified primary comparison before secondary findings, and the conclusion answer that same question. Give arm-level absolute results and the between-group estimate with its interval when available and appropriate, and material harms when needed to interpret benefit. A between-group benefit can mean less deterioration, not improvement from baseline. Do not present failure to show superiority as equivalence or better discrimination as clinical benefit. For diagnostic studies identify the index test and reference standard; for prediction distinguish development, validation and clinical utility; for observational work retain the association and its limits. Adapt to other article types; never invent results for a protocol or missing data.

## Reconcile and deliver

Check the two readings against the manuscript, protocol or confirmed facts **after** noting the reader's interpretation. If the same editor already saw the manuscript while drafting/reviewing, call this a reader-perspective check, not an independent blind test. If true blinding is requested and feasible, give a fresh reader only the title, lock their interpretation before revealing sources, and keep reviewer reports isolated. Do not claim ignorance of a recognized study.

Decide **keep / minimally revise / author confirmation**. For a substantive issue report the triggering words, plausible misreading, verified fact and smallest correction. Without a source, label the possible ambiguity; do not resolve it by guessing. Honor review-only requests. Keep diagnostics in the response rather than the clean text. For a clear passage, no forced rewrite; for a revision, repeat the two separate readings and check that brevity has not changed the design, outcome or conclusion.

## Published examples for calibration, not templates

For an offline illustration of a misleading design, comparator or endpoint, use the [synthetic paired examples](writing-example-cards.md). No external reading is needed to use those cards.

- [JAMA: screening outreach](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2652650). Naming outreach and completion keeps the title from implying a comparison of the tests' diagnostic accuracy.
- [The Lancet: DiRECT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29221645/). Primary care, diabetes remission and cluster randomization identify the question; the operational definition of remission belongs in the abstract.
- [NEJM: early Alzheimer's disease](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2212948). The short drug-and-disease title does not identify randomization. Learn from the abstract's scale direction and arm-specific changes, not from that omission: less decline is not recovery.
